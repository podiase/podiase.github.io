Towards Understanding and Modeling Neural Expectations in Interactive Systems

What happens in the brain when a system does something we didn't expect? In a simulated driving task, unannounced steering-wheel interventions produce real surprise. We capture it with 31-channel EEG, facial-affect tracking, and pupillometry.

What Should the Answer Accomplish? Goal Selection in Medical AI

Two medically correct answers can still serve a patient differently. We map the goals physicians pursue when they answer the same question, then build a system that infers a patient's response goals from their story and conditions the AI's answer on them.

About

My work intersects medicine, neuroscience, and computing, building technology around the needs of real patients.

At Carnegie Mellon, I have been studying how people form expectations of interactive systems, using EEG and other physiological signals, and how patient-facing AI can decide what an answer should accomplish, not just whether it is correct.

My earlier research focused on the neurobiology of neurodegenerative disease: how dopamine signaling is regulated, studied in C. elegans, in human cells, and through population studies of cholesterol-lowering medication. Alongside the lab, I have long designed and built for people, from front-end development and user testing on a patient portal at Allscripts to freelance web design and machine learning for health analytics. I now hope to bring these together, using sensing, interactive systems, and AI to study neurodegenerative disease and improve care for the people living with it.

I graduated as valedictorian of Fisk University. Outside the lab, I build clinics, training programs, and educational centers in impoverished communities, and I advocate for global malaria funding.
